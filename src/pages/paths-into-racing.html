Getting started

How much does it cost to start racing? Every route, costed end to end

Nobody is ready for a Hoosier Super Tour the Monday after a license school. A license is a credential; speed and racecraft are hours. These are two different clocks, and almost all of the money follows the second one. Here is what each route actually costs, what you are holding at the end of it, and how to pick.

Every figure below is dated, sourced and reproducible. Where a competitor does not publish a number, we say so rather than estimating one.

The two clocks

The license is the cheap part

The lowest-priced school we found that ends in a Full Competition License with a car supplied is MSR Houston. Their three-day school in a rented Spec Racer Ford Gen2 is $4,450, and about $5,400 with SCCA membership, the license fee and travel (we costed the travel from Huntsville; yours will differ). If a Novice Permit is enough to start, PTC's two-day school in Fayetteville, TN is $3,625 with the car included. We rent Spec Racer Fords and we will still point you to a school if the license is all you want. Every school's price is on the license page.

But look at what you are holding when you walk out: a school certificate for a license, and somewhere between three and ten hours of track time. Getting a license is the easy part. Getting good is the hard part, and it is also the fun part.

The Hoosier Super Tour at Barber costs $795 to enter, requires a Full license, and has no test day. The gap between those two paragraphs is what this page is about. In plain terms: how much does it cost to start racing, and how much does it cost to be ready to race?

The journey

Progress comes in plateaus

Getting good in a race car does not happen in a straight line. It comes in plateaus: stretches where it feels like nothing is changing, then a jump. The license is one small step in there. Here is roughly what the plateaus look like and how much seat time each one usually takes. You can start on the first one today with SCCA membership and a driver's license.

  • Plateau 1

    At home in the car. Track days and Time Trials: a real clock, no wheel-to-wheel. Most drivers stop thinking about the car and start thinking about the track within a handful of track weekends.

  • Plateau 2

    Racing, and finishing. A school and a Novice Permit get you onto a regional grid. The first few race weekends are about starts, traffic and bringing the car home; most people feel settled in a regional field after three to five of them.

  • Plateau 3

    Within a second of the fast drivers. This is pure seat time, and it is attainable: one to two years of steady running gets most committed drivers there. It's the most satisfying jump on the list, because the stopwatch shows it.

  • Plateau 4

    Racecraft. Strategy, tire management, where to pass and when not to, what to do on lap one with a full field around you. This takes longer than the speed did, and it is what separates a quick lap from a good result at a Major, a Super Tour or the Runoffs.

  • And on

    Your own measure. For some people that's a regional championship, for some a national podium, for some just a cleaner lap at their home track than last year. It's all progress, and every hour in the car adds to it.

What that looks like in real results

Ryan Starck races our SRF3 and came to the sport as an adult. In his earliest results we can find, late 2024 and spring 2025, he was roughly two seconds a lap off the fastest SRF3s. By summer 2025 he was within about a second at Road Atlanta, and at Atlanta Motorsports Park that June he set the SRF3 lap record and still finished third in class. That is plateau three arriving, and plateau four showing up right behind it: the fastest lap is not the same thing as the win. In 2026 he took on the Hoosier Super Tour, with fields of 38 to 47 cars, and finished anywhere from 14th to 39th, with his best result, 14th of 40, coming at Barber in August. Nothing about that is a setback. It is exactly what the journey looks like.

A teenage driver from the Atlanta area finished SCCA drivers' school in March 2025, won an SRF3 race at a SEDIV Major about 15 months later in a seven-car field, and then lined up for his first Runoffs that October, finishing 24th of 44, about five seconds a lap off the fastest laps. Quick in a small field, then a big step up against the best in the country: another plateau, another thing to learn.

The honest trade

The schools are cheaper. Here's what the difference buys.

A school day in a PTC, MSR Houston or Hallett car costs less than renting ours and towing it to a track. The arithmetic is two sections up. What matters is what the difference buys you.

Seat time in the car you race

A school puts you in their car for two or three days, and many schools teach in a formula car or a sports car you are unlikely to race. The SRF3 is a sealed spec car: one SRF3 drives like another, and your own seat insert moves with you to any of them. So every hour in ours, from a Monday test to a Super Tour, builds on the last. Nothing resets.

The same crew, who know that car

The people who set the car up at Roebling are the people who set it up at Barber, and they remember what you asked for last time. When a customer put a rear corner into the wall at Mid-Ohio on a wet Saturday, the car was repaired and on grid Sunday morning.

Access to qualified coaches

Renting from us means access to our coaches, Cliff White and Mark Eaton, and to coaches in our network who know this car: Evan Slater, Morgan Burkhard and Dave Ogburn. At race weekends the debrief between sessions is part of it: data, video and a plan for the next session. Track days and time trials are arranged in advance for drivers who want to keep progressing; say so when you book and we'll build it in.

No truck, no trailer, no prep weekend

Be clear about who this is a difference from. A school also hands you a prepared car, so against a school it is a wash. Against owning your own SRF it is the whole difference: the car is transported, prepped, fuelled, run and put away, and you drive to the track. For anyone with a job, that often decides it more than the money does.

Read the fine print first

The costs nobody quotes you

Most of these are ours to be straight about, and one is an industry problem.

Damage

The biggest unpublished cost in this category

Most racing schools say only that the student is "responsible for damages." Ours is in the open. Damage you cause is yours to pay for; a mechanical failure that is not your fault is not. The $6,000 crash-damage deposit is held while you race with us, and it is security, not a limit.

We show what that means in practice, with the common crashes we see and how each repair is billed: what a typical crash costs.

Safety gear

$1,000–1,600 for a legal minimum kit

Suit, helmet, head-and-neck restraint, arm restraints, shoes and gloves. It is a one-time cost that travels with you to every car and every track, so buy it once and buy it well.

We wrote the whole thing up, including what the GCR actually requires and where to buy it: buying safety gear.

Quotes

You'll be quoted differently everywhere, except here

On this site you can build a detailed, line-by-line quote yourself with the rental calculator. Almost everywhere else you call or submit a form, and the price that comes back grows once the number of days and the towing distance are added in.

We take that risk away by publishing how we price.

SCCA fees

Published, and small next to the driving

SCCA membership is $85 a year plus region dues. A Novice Permit is $150 and a Full Competition License is $125, both bought in SCCA's online store at my.scca.com once a school signs you off.

Call SCCA on 1-800-770-2055 if anything about your case is unusual.

Tires and fuel

Estimated up front, settled on what you use

We have run Spec Racer Fords long enough to know what they cost, down to these details. Our estimates assume scuffed Hoosiers, which is what most people run; a new set across a Barber weekend adds around $850.

You can always ask for a different tire strategy: new Hoosiers, used Hoosiers, or Toyo R888Rs, which last longer and run slower lap times.

Transport and prep

Every line is a specific job

Get ready: prep is getting the car ready, aligning it and fitting you in it. Get there: transport, charged once per event. Rent the car, by the hour on track. We do the work: the trackside crew, per day. You come, have fun and learn along the way.

That is why a single day looks expensive and a two-day weekend doesn't. Our benchmark two-day track weekend at Barber, two cars in the trailer, is $4,533.66, around $4,500.
